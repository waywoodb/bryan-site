Fifty years in steel.
Now, clay.

Bryan Mackenzie spent a lifetime bending steel into spiral staircases. These days he fires porcelain in a wood-fired kiln he built on the factory floor — chasing colour, transparency, and the occasional glorious failure.

An idea in steel

In 1974, a fitter and turner in inner-north Melbourne wanted a staircase down to the cellar of his house. He couldn't buy a spiral stair in Australia — so he designed one.

1975 — Gertrude Street, FitzroyThat staircase became enzie. Friends wanted one, then their friends did, and within a year there was a showroom on Gertrude Street with the factory squeezed in behind it. The design was modular steel: simple enough, and precise enough, that a renovator could assemble a whole staircase in a day. That is still how an enzie stair arrives.

1980s — RecognitionThe Australian Design Award followed, then the BHP Award for Consumer Products and a finalist's place in the Prince Philip Prize for Australian Design. The workshop outgrew Fitzroy for Fairfield, distributors were appointed through Singapore, Hong Kong and Japan, and the stairs have been drawn, cut and folded in Melbourne ever since — on CNC routers and laser scanners now, but still in steel.

Will it hold?The question he has always built towards. There is a photograph pinned to his Instagram under exactly that title: a helical stair shot from directly above, with a crowd standing shoulder to shoulder on every tread, all the way down to the floor. It held.

Now — Fitzroy NorthBryan still lives where it started. He and his wife Marija — both makers — rebuilt on their Fitzroy North block for the long haul and put a chrome-yellow enzie spiral at the centre of it. He chose that stair for its colour, and for the way light comes through a perforated tread. Which is, as it turns out, exactly what he is chasing now — only in a different material.

The work

Celadon

The green that potters have chased for a thousand years. Bryan's celadons pool soft and deep in the bellies of hand-built bowls — sage through jade to olive — each one worked out against a row of small test chips lined up on the bench like samples in a toolroom. Old habits from a life of precision engineering die hard.

No two fire the same. That's the point.

Colour & transparency

Cobalt, cornflower, chrome yellow, lemon, cream — glazed thin enough that the light gets into them. Behind the bench sit the jars they come from: felspar, silica, whiting, kaolin, dolomite, bentonite, every recipe numbered and labelled in Bryan's hand like a part on a drawing.

These are pieces for the table, made to be used daily and looked through as much as at.

The table, assembled

Cornflower plates, bottle-green vessels, grey and sage bowls that sit together the way a good room does — nothing matching, everything belonging. Bryan builds these settings piece by piece, and the oval wall platters from the same family hang as quiet colour fields in their own right.

Commissions

Most of what Bryan makes now, he makes for someone in particular.

A setting built up over several firings for one long table. A run of bowls in a single glaze for a restaurant. A platter sized and coloured for a wall you already have in mind.

Tell him the story behind it, too — the anniversary, the house you have just finished, the person it is really for, the green off a hillside you grew up on. That is the part Bryan works with. It comes back to you in the glaze he mixes for it, the size he throws to, the shape he keeps returning to until it is right. A piece made for a reason ends up a different object from a piece made to a specification.

From there it is a conversation, usually with a test chip or two in the post. Be generous with the timing: glazes take the time they take, and a wood-fired kiln has opinions of its own.

The firing

A kiln in a stair factory

The firing happens where the steel does. On the floor of the enzie factory, between the racks of tube and the CNC machines, Bryan laid the bricks for a wood-fired jet kiln by hand — lots of trial, and plenty of error.

Fifty years ago he built the thing he couldn't buy. He still does.
